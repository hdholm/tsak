The Supreme Court needs independence. A justice has to be able to tell a
president, Congress, or an angry majority that the law doesn't allow what
they want to do. It also needs legitimacy. People should be able to believe
that cases are decided on legal judgment, not private favors, undisclosed
relationships, or one party's success at manipulating the appointment
calendar.

We have too often treated those goals as opposites. Accountability gets
described as an attack on independence, and lifetime tenure gets treated as
proof of it. Neither of those is true.

We should keep the current nine justice size of the bench, establish
staggered eighteen-year terms with one scheduled appointment every two
years, and create enforceable ethics oversight. None of that ends
disagreement about constitutional interpretation. It would make the
institution less dependent on accident, personal discretion, and
tactical advantage.

The Court also sits downstream of the rest of this site. It decides what
[campaign finance rules](../money-in-politics/) we may have, and it
shapes the limits of [election and districting law](../representation/).
Who appoints its members, and on what schedule, matters to all of it.

## Where We Started

### Independence was a deliberate choice

Article III says federal judges hold office during good behavior and
protects their pay from being cut. The aim is to insulate judgment from
political punishment.[^constitution] The president nominates and the Senate
gives advice and consent. The Constitution doesn't say nine. Congress set
the Court at nine in 1869, after earlier changes.[^court]

So there are two different questions. The number of seats is a matter of
statute. The tenure of the people in them raises a constitutional issue.

Independence serves a public purpose: it should protect a justice who
makes an unpopular decision. It should not shield conduct that has
nothing to do with the merits from ordinary scrutiny.

### Lifetime service became an appointment lottery

Under the current arrangement a vacancy depends on death, illness, or
the timing of a retirement. Presidents who serve the same number of
years can get very different chances to shape the Court. Younger
nominees promise longer influence. A justice thinking about retirement
knows the party in control may decide the replacement. A vacancy near
an election becomes a crisis because its effects can last decades.

Not every retirement is strategic nor every nomination cynical. But
the rules invite calculation even from people acting in good faith.

## Where We Are

### The rules change with the advantage

In March 2016 President Obama nominated Merrick Garland after Justice
Scalia's death. The Republican-controlled Senate Judiciary Committee never
considered the nomination. In October 2020, after Justice Ginsburg's death,
a Republican-controlled Senate confirmed President Trump's nominee, Amy
Coney Barrett, shortly before the presidential election.[^senate][^barrett]

| Vacancy | Senate action | Institutional lesson |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2016: Garland nomination | No Judiciary Committee consideration; no confirmation | A majority can hold a vacancy open for a possible change in presidential control. |
| 2020: Barrett nomination | Confirmed October 26, before the November election | Election proximity did not prevent action when the majority supported the nominating president. |

Republican leaders explained the difference by whether the same party
held the presidency and the Senate. That is a political
distinction. It is not a neutral, constitutionally prescribed
election-year timetable.[^hearing]

Both parties escalated. Senate Democrats lowered the cloture threshold
for lower-court nominees in 2013, and Republicans extended the change
to Supreme Court nominees in 2017.[^senate] The decisions, contexts,
and consequences weren't the same, and I won't pretend they were. But
the pattern is plain: procedural restraints weaken when a majority
sees an immediate benefit in discarding them.

The Constitution sets no confirmation deadline, so any reform has to
deal with obstruction as well as vacancy timing. Regular terms without
a workable process would just give us regularly scheduled standoffs.

### Ethical concerns deserve more than reassurance

Reports of expensive travel, gifts, outside income, and undisclosed
relationships have raised legitimate questions about how the justices
manage conflicts. In December 2024 the Senate Judiciary Committee's
Democratic majority staff issued an investigative report criticizing
disclosure and recusal practices and calling for enforceable
rules.[^investigation] That is an investigative and partisan source,
not a court judgment on every alleged violation, and it should be read
alongside the underlying evidence and the responses. Still, the public
shouldn't have to rely only on a justice's own assurance.

Justice Clarence Thomas's travel financed by Harlan Crow is one
example.  Associated Press reporting in June 2024 described additional
trips identified by Senate investigators. Thomas defended his
disclosure practice on the basis of advice that hospitality from close
friends didn't need to be reported.[^thomas] That is a dispute about
disclosure obligations. It doesn't show that a vote was purchased. It
does show why independent evaluation is more credible than leaving the
dispute to the person involved. At the **very** least several of the
justices have catastrophically failed to avoid *the appearance of
impropriety* which is a standard to which many other officials are
held and which should be the bare minimum for the country's highest
court.

The real ethical question is bigger than bribery. A judge may
sincerely believe a friendship has no influence while reasonable
observers see a troubling relationship. Appearance matters because
litigants can't inspect a judge's private reasoning.

### A code exists; enforcement is the question

The Court adopted a written Code of Conduct in November 2023, covering
impartiality, political activity, outside activities, and
recusal.[^code] The weakness is accountability. The code creates no
independent complaint-and-adjudication process, and a rule that
depends on each justice's own assessment is thin comfort when that
judgement is the thing in dispute.

Federal law already covers disqualification. Section 455 of Title 28
applies to justices as well as other federal judges, including
situations involving impartiality and financial interests.[^recusal]
The question is how anyone credibly evaluates compliance at the
highest court.

There has been useful administrative progress. In February 2026 the
Court announced software for automated conflict checks, backed by
revised filing rules effective in March.[^software] Catching conflicts
earlier helps.  Software doesn't decide contested ethical questions or
provide independent enforcement.

## Alternatives and the Principles They Suggest

### Protect judgment without making service indefinite

The choice isn't lifetime tenure versus judges campaigning for
reelection. A long, nonrenewable term protects a justice from
immediate political pressure and still gives predictable
turnover. Eighteen years spans several administrations and
congressional majorities. Making it nonrenewable removes any
temptation to please whoever could grant another term. An eighteen
year is a significant part of a career for most people, and should be
long enough to spend in one job.

The Brennan Center's eighteen-year proposal and the term-limit bills
in Congress show this is a developed idea, not just an appealing round
number.[^terms][^bill]

Regular appointments would also reduce the premium on the youngest
possible nominee. Experience could count for more when appointing a
forty-five-year-old rather than a sixty-year-old no longer promises
fifteen more years of control.

### Oversight should review conduct, not punish decisions

An ethics system has to separate conflicts, gifts, disclosure
failures, and misuse of office from disagreement with a ruling. If
elected officials could sanction a justice for an inconvenient
decision, independence would be destroyed. But treating every
investigation as retaliation would make real standards impossible. A
conflict rule is valuable exactly when the person subject to it would
prefer not to step aside.

The test is whether the process is neutral, evidence-based, and
constrained.  Who may complain? Who investigates? What standard
applies? Is there a right to respond? Are conclusions explained? Can
the process itself be reviewed?

## Potential Solutions

### Nine seats, one regular appointment every two years

Establishing nine active seats with fixed, staggered eighteen-year
terms is a well thought out proposal that should be enacted. In the
mature system one term would end every two years, so a president
serving a full four-year term would ordinarily make two nominations,
subject to confirmation.[^terms]

![Proposed appointment schedule: nine fixed slots come due at years 2,
4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, and 18; each succeeding full term lasts
eighteen years.](court-terms.svg)

*Proposed steady-state schedule, not a transition calendar for the
current justices. Vacancies and delayed confirmations need separate
rules.*

That spreads appointment opportunities more evenly. It would not turn
the Court into a mirror of the latest election, and it shouldn't;
constitutional adjudication needs distance from short-term
politics. We should also bar renewal or extension of the active term,
and avoid post-Court arrangements that let a justice's next employer
create conflicts in the final years. With a functional retirement
system that I believe already exists, even significant restrictions on
post-term empolyment should not be constraining after an 18 year
term. A judicial appointment should carry a lifetime benefit, it
need not be a path to exteme wealth.

### Make the difficult details explicit

"Eighteen years" as a slogan leaves a lot unanswered:

- **Early vacancies:** a replacement should fill the remainder of the seat's
  term rather than restart the clock. Otherwise an early departure recreates
  the lottery.
- **Obstruction:** nominations need a defined process for hearings and a
  decision. A deadline is worthless unless the consequence of missing it is
  specified.
- **Delayed confirmation:** the seat schedule should continue. Delay
  shouldn't produce a longer term or an extra future appointment.
- **The Chief Justice:** the chief's administrative role needs its own clear
  selection and term rules, not an exception that swallows the reform.
- **Transition:** current service, future appointments, and the start of
  staggering need a published plan adopted before anyone knows its partisan
  results.

There is a tradeoff. Filling an early vacancy still gives a president
an extra nomination, even if the long-run schedule is unchanged. No
honest proposal can promise that unexpected deaths stop
mattering. This particular situation is no worse than the current
status quo.

For confirmation obstruction we need an amendment specifying an
enforceable decision process while keeping a real Senate role. Silence
shouldn't be a permanent veto, and confirmation shouldn't be automatic
because a calendar ran out. The fallback has to be designed, not left
to partisan improvisation. This can likely be legislatively adopted
early with an intent for constitutional ammendment to prevent
politically motivated changes to the process.

### Use a route that can withstand challenge

Some term-limit proposals keep lifetime judicial office but limit
regular Supreme Court service, with senior status
afterward. Supporters say Congress can do that by statute. Opponents
dispute whether Congress can redefine the office that
way.[^terms][^bill]

**The ideal is a constitutional amendment for fixed terms.** It is the
harder political route, but it deals directly with Article III and
lets the appointment schedule and the anti-obstruction provisions be
adopted together. An amendment needs proposal and ratification under
Article V.[^constitution] Difficulty is not a reason to pretend an
ordinary statute is unquestionably enough. It also isn't a reason to
put off ethics work and pure legislative implementation in the meantime.

### Build an ethics system the public can inspect

![Proposed ethics process: a complaint or conflict flag receives
independent screening, an investigation with a right to respond where
warranted, and a reasoned decision with review. Decisions concern
conduct rather than the merits of rulings.](court-ethics.svg)

*Proposed oversight structure. Its legal authority and remedies would
need to be defined consistently with judicial independence and
constitutional removal provisions.*

I would require timely, searchable disclosures; strict limits on gifts and
privately financed travel; clear rules on investments and outside income;
and written explanations of significant recusal decisions.

An independent ethics office should be able to receive complaints,
screen out unsupported ones, obtain information, and investigate
substantiated concerns. A separate, carefully constituted review body
should decide contested matters, so the subject of a complaint doesn't
control the outcome. A justice should get notice, access to the
evidence, a chance to respond, and a defined route for review, and the
system needs protection against repetitive complaints aimed at keeping
someone out of a particular case.

Remedies must fit the authority: corrected disclosures, published
findings, lawful recusal procedures, and referral for further
action. Ethics administrators shouldn't get an invented power to
remove Article III judges.  Impeachment stays a constitutional
process, not a substitute for everyday oversight.

This connects to [money in
politics](../money-in-politics/). Concentrated wealth can influence
institutions without buying a vote outright, and a public system
should guard against privileged access and hidden dependence before
anyone has to prove an explicit bargain. A Court that can enforce
limits on everyone else should accept limits on itself. Independence
and accountability reinforce each other when both are designed to
serve the public.
