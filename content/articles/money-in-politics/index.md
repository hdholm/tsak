Every citizen has one vote. We do not have the same ability to put an
issue on the agenda, fund a campaign, or keep an argument in front of
the public.  Some of that is unavoidable. People have different
skills, audiences, and commitments. But persuasion earned through
public support is not the same as influence bought with a fortune, and
a democracy ought to tell the difference.

We need to reverse the constitutional rule from *Citizens United*,
along with broader campaign-finance reform. Of course, people must
have the right to criticize government, organize, publish unpopular
arguments, and challenge the people in office. The design problem is
protecting those freedoms without treating unlimited money as an
untouchable part of political liberty.

This is closely tied to [who draws the maps and counts the
votes](../representation/), to [how concentrated economic power
protects itself](../economic-policy/), and to [who sits on the Supreme
Court](../supreme-court/), which decides what campaign finance rules
we are allowed to have.

## Where We Started

### Money helps make speech possible

Printing a leaflet costs money. So does a website, a meeting hall, or
an advertisement. A law controlling political spending can therefore
control opportunities to communicate.

That is the serious free-speech argument against careless regulation,
and it should not be dismissed just because a wealthy speaker invokes
it. A rule that restricts a corporation today can be turned on a
union, a neighborhood group, or a dissident publisher tomorrow. But we
need to remind ourselves over and over, as our political system has
not, that corporations are ***not*** people. People have rights
endowed by their creator. They have thougts and feelings and
desires. Corporations do not, and we far too often confuse the two.

But campaigns also need money to compete, and the people who can
supply it in large amounts get opportunities ordinary constituents
don't. We have to protect expression and resist financial dependence
at the same time.

### The doctrine did not begin in 2010

In *Buckley v. Valeo* (1976) the Supreme Court separated contributions
from expenditures. It upheld important contribution limits and struck
down several limits on spending, including independent expenditures
and a candidate's spending of personal funds.[^buckley]

A contribution hands resources to a campaign. An independent
expenditure buys political advocacy outside the campaign, without
coordination. Both can move an election, but the law treats them
differently. So reform can't be reduced to one decision or the slogan
that money is speech.

## Where We Are

### What Citizens United actually changed

In *Citizens United v. FEC* (2010) the Court struck down restrictions
on corporate independent expenditures and electioneering
communications. The reasoning covers unions too. It left the
disclosure and disclaimer requirements alone and did not touch the ban
on corporations giving directly to candidates.[^citizens]

So no, corporations cannot write unlimited checks to a federal
candidate.  Nor did the decision invent spending by wealthy
individuals; those protections already existed.

The Court's central premise was that independent expenditures don't
create enough corruption, or appearance of corruption, to justify the
restriction.  That is far too narrow. A donor doesn't have to buy a
particular vote to gain influence. Politicians know who can help them,
who can fund a challenger, and who makes campaigning
affordable. Access and dependence shape which proposals advance long
before anyone casts a roll call vote.

### Super PACs required another legal step

The D.C. Circuit's *SpeechNow.org v. FEC* decision, also in 2010,
struck down contribution limits for groups that make only independent
expenditures.  Combined with *Citizens United* and later rulemaking,
that created the independent-expenditure-only committee we call the
super PAC.[^speechnow][^rules]

A super PAC can take unlimited contributions from lawful sources. It
can't give to candidates or coordinate in a way that makes it a
prohibited or excessive contribution. "Independent" is a legal
term. It does not mean the spender has no preference or no
relationship to a candidate.[^independent] That is why enforcing the
coordination rules matters. If a supposedly separate operation is an
extension of a campaign, the label shouldn't be enough.

### Disclosure is necessary but not enough

Disclosure lets voters ask who paid for a message. It gives
journalists and watchdogs something to investigate, and it does so
without the government deciding which viewpoint is right.

But disclosure after the election is worth much less than disclosure
while decisions are being made. And naming an organization often
doesn't reveal where its money came from. Reform should make election
funding traceable through intermediary entities. It should also
protect people who face a demonstrable risk of harassment, using
thresholds and clear scope rather than a public dossier on every small
donor. But again, corporations, PACs, unions, etc., are ***not
people*** with thoughts, viewpoints, and moral consciences. People
make choices and dollars should be traceable to people. There are
certainly reasons for anonymity and some allowances should be made,
but that should not allow the hiding of vast sums of money.

Mostly, though, transparency doesn't equalize anything. Learning that
a billionaire paid for an ad campaign doesn't give an ordinary voter
the means to answer it.

### Influence reaches beyond election advertising

Lobbying, litigation, policy research, the revolving door, and
ownership of communication platforms all shape government. Not all of
it is corrupt; organized advocacy is a normal part of public life. The
problem is concentrated capacity. An industry can keep teams of
specialists on the payroll while the people who bear its costs have
little time, money, or information to respond.

Robert Reich argues that wealth shapes the rules of the market, so
that political influence and economic advantage reinforce each
other.[^reich] That is a political-economic argument, not a claim that
every lobbying contact is illegal. He is largely right, and [the
economic policy article](../economic-policy/) follows that feedback
loop. Campaign finance is one part of a larger question: who gets an
effective voice when the rules are written?

## Alternatives and the Principles They Suggest

### Make ordinary participation financially meaningful

New York City's matching-funds program multiplies eligible small
contributions from residents. Seattle's Democracy Voucher Program
gives eligible residents public money to direct to participating
candidates.[^nyc][^seattle]

They have different rules and operate in particular local settings, so
a national program can't just copy a city's administrative
details. They do show that public financing can strengthen
participation rather than just cap spending.

![Illustration of an eight-to-one small-donor match: a qualifying $25
contribution receives $200 in public matching funds, making $225
available to the participating campaign.](small-donor-match.svg)

*Arithmetic illustration using NYC's matching rate. Actual
eligibility, qualification requirements, matchable amounts, and
payment caps apply. It is not an unconditional payment for every

A candidate who can fund a competitive campaign with many modest
contributions has a real alternative to courting a few wealthy
backers. That changes who gets listened to.

### Protect collective advocacy

People speak through organizations. Unions, civil-rights groups,
churches, professional associations, and newspapers pool resources
because one person can't do everything alone. A reform that bans
organized speech would damage democracy. So would one that exempts
every incorporated entity without asking how its election spending is
financed. But again, people matter. Organizations should be a
reflection of their people and a handful of powerful donars should not
be able to hide behind an "organization" that is nothing more that a
name.

Consistent treatment should be a central principle, not a list of favored
speakers. Rules should target defined election-finance conduct, ignore
viewpoint, and protect independent journalism and ordinary issue
advocacy.  The boundaries are hard. An issue ad can affect an
election, and a newspaper editorial can endorse a
candidate. Legislators must not be allowed to turn those facts into a
general power to suppress criticism.

## Potential Solutions

### Reverse the rule through a legitimate constitutional route

Congress can't nullify a constitutional holding by reenacting the same
restriction under a new name. A future Court could reconsider its
precedent, which is one more reason [who sits on the
Court](../supreme-court/) matters. The more durable route is a
constitutional amendment authorizing reasonable regulation of election
contributions and spending.[^constitution]

One possibility is a narrowly drawn amendment that permits that regulation
while expressly protecting political viewpoints, independent journalism, and
people organizing to advocate for change. Its purpose should be preventing
corruption, dependence, and domination of elections. It should not let
government decide that one argument has had enough attention.

It also has to cover corporations, unions, and wealthy individuals alike.
Leaving unlimited individual spending untouched while changing only
corporate spending would fix one mechanism and leave the others.

### Reversal doesn't fix everything

Overruling *Citizens United* would not erase *Buckley*, *SpeechNow*,
or every related rule. A serious proposal has to say which spending
can be regulated, which contributions can be limited, and how
organizations are classified.  Nor would reversal cure polarization,
make candidates honest, or end unequal media ownership. That is a
reason to define the proposal honestly, not to give up on it.

### Work on reforms that don't require waiting

We should pursue a parallel package:

- Timely, usable disclosure of significant election spending and its
  sources, with protection where disclosure creates a substantiated risk.
  This should trace to individual citizens and once again only people,
  not corporations or unions, make decisions and should have free
  speach rights.
- Real enforcement of coordination and source restrictions.
- Voluntary public financing built around broad participation, small
  contributions, or voter-directed vouchers.
- Clear conflict-of-interest rules for officials, and transparency about
  lobbying and the move between government and regulated industries.
- An enforcement process that decides things in time, rather than letting
  delay work as permission.

The details must fit constitutional limits. In *Arizona Free
Enterprise Club v. Bennett* (2011) the Court struck down a
public-funding mechanism that increased a candidate's subsidy when
opponents or independent groups spent more. Matching a qualifying
small donation is different. Good intentions don't make every funding
formula lawful or effective.[^arizona]

### Protect reform from the people administering it

The government will be enforcing rules that affect criticism of the
government. That conflict can't be avoided, only managed. I would insist on
precise definitions, public decisions, enforceable deadlines, independent
review, and safeguards against selective enforcement. A small civic
organization should be able to understand its obligations without a
permanent legal department.

Public financing needs audits and anti-fraud controls, and candidates should
show real public support before receiving serious money. Then measure it:
who participates, whether candidates have credible alternatives to large
donors, and whether enforcement falls hardest on small groups.

The point is not to make everyone's speech identical in reach. It is to
build a democracy in which extraordinary wealth doesn't routinely decide
whose concerns can sustain a campaign. Freedom of speech protects our
ability to argue about the country's future. It shouldn't require us to
give up every practical safeguard against buying control of it.
