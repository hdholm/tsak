A healthcare system should help people stay well, catch problems early, and
manage illness before it becomes a crisis. When serious illness does come, it
should provide excellent treatment without making the illness a financial
catastrophe. That seems like a modest expectation for a wealthy country.

Yet Americans regularly face a different set of questions. Is the doctor in
the network? Will the insurer authorize the treatment? How much of the
deductible is left? Can the appointment wait until the next paycheck? Is
there still a hospital close enough in an emergency?

We have extraordinary medical knowledge, skilled clinicians, and institutions
capable of remarkable work. We also have a financing system that too often
turns reaching them into an obstacle course. The result is too much **sick
care**: responding after people become seriously ill, while the ordinary work
of keeping them healthier is hard to get and badly funded.

**We should begin with Medicare for All: universal public insurance, a
comprehensive benefit, and properly funded primary care available before a
problem becomes an emergency.** It is a foundation, not the whole building.
Prevention, continuing care, and hospital treatment still have to work
together.

## Where We Started

### Coverage accumulated in layers

Nobody designed a coherent national system and then put it into operation. We
accumulated arrangements for different groups: employment-based insurance,
individual policies, public programs, and a safety net for people who didn't
fit anywhere. Medicare and Medicaid arrived in 1965 to meet different needs
through different structures. Later reforms expanded coverage and added
protections without replacing the patchwork.[^history]

That is why changing jobs, crossing an eligibility threshold, or moving can
change your coverage even though your medical needs haven't changed. The
system sorts people by how they qualify for payment instead of by what care
they need.

Insurance is supposed to pool risk. Illness is unpredictable for an
individual, but a population can finance care together. The question
isn't whether we pool risk; we already do. It is how many pools we
have, who can get into them, and how much effort goes into moving
money among them.  Additionally, health risk is increasingly more
determinable than controllable.  When an insurance provider can
determine based on someone's current health, genetic makeup,
neighborhood, and profession how likely they are to need expensive
care, an individual can find themselves priced out of healthcare, even
care to help mitigate those risks because of factors outside their
control.  That's not pooled risk, that's high-stakes gambling with the
insurance industry house always in a position to win.

### We built payment around episodes, not health

It is easier to bill for a procedure than for the patient who never
deteriorated because a clinician noticed a problem, adjusted
treatment, or coordinated follow-up. When those quieter services are
poorly funded, a system rewards responding to illness more reliably
than preventing it.

Healthcare includes prevention, timely diagnosis, treatment,
rehabilitation, and the continuing work of living with chronic
disease. Primary care is the usual source of care across all of that,
as AHRQ's discussion of primary care and patient safety
emphasizes.[^primarycare] "Sick care" describes an imbalance: we wait
until a problem is severe, then deploy expensive resources.  Emergency
departments, specialists, and intensive care units do indispensable
work. They shouldn't have to make up for a primary care system people
can't reach.

### We confused insurance choice with medical choice

Choosing among insurers is not choosing a physician. A person can have
several plans available and find that each excludes their doctor or
puts up a different barrier. An insurance card also doesn't create a
clinic. Financing and delivery are separate responsibilities, and a
workable reform has to address both.

## Where We Are

### We spend enough to expect better

CMS reports national health spending reached **$5.3 trillion in
2024**, or **$15,474 per person**, **18.0 percent of GDP**. That is
national expenditure, not just insurer revenue or federal
spending.[^spending]

![Three measures of U.S. national health expenditure in 2024: $5.3
trillion total, $15,474 per person, and 18.0 percent of
GDP.](healthcare-spending.svg)

*Source: CMS National Health Expenditure Accounts, calendar year
2024. These are three descriptions of the same spending, not amounts
to add together.*

The Commonwealth Fund's *Mirror, Mirror 2024* ranked the United States
last overall among ten high-income countries, across access, care
processes, administrative efficiency, equity, and outcomes. We did
relatively well on care processes and poorly on most of the
rest.[^mirror] A ranking isn't a complete explanation. Country
selection, measures, and weighting matter, and population health also
reflects housing, inequality, violence, and diet. But spending this
much while leaving this many obstacles to care should make us question
the design.

To be fair, not every U.S. outcome is getting worse every year. CDC
reports life expectancy rose from 78.4 years in 2023 to 79.0 in
2024.[^mortality] That is welcome. But there are many factors and it
does not appear that this is a return to our historical trend of
increasing life expectancy. Nor does it mean we're doing well compared
to peer countries---we're not.[^hst] It's also worth noting that life
expectancy is not the same as quality life years.[^WHOGHOHALE]
Although those numbers also seem to show us well behind peer
countries, with our averages showing fluctuation, but no real
sustained growth in the last 20 years.

### Delayed care moves the burden downstream

When an appointment, prescription, or follow-up feels unaffordable, people
postpone it. The need doesn't go away with the bill. A manageable condition
worsens until urgent treatment is unavoidable.

For people with diabetes, heart failure, or chronic lung disease,
reliable monitoring, medication, and continuing care can head off
deterioration. AHRQ uses "potentially preventable" hospitalizations
for these conditions as a way to examine access to outpatient
care. The word "potentially" matters: it doesn't mean every admission
could have been avoided or that patients are to blame.[^preventable]
The case is for supporting chronic care throughout an illness instead
of repeatedly paying to rescue people after a crisis.

We should expect more routine chronic care once people can finally get
it.  The reduction we want is in avoidable complications and the
emergency or intensive treatment they cause. Living well with a
chronic condition takes years of continuing care; success means
supporting it consistently, not pretending the condition is gone.

![Two conceptual pathways: financial barriers and weak primary care
can contribute to delayed management and avoidable crises; universal
affordable coverage with funded primary care supports earlier
assessment and continuing management, with the aim of reducing
avoidable deterioration.](healthcare-before-crisis.svg)

*A proposed shift in the timing of care, not a forecast of how much
hospital use would fall. Prevention and continuing care cannot
eliminate every illness, emergency, or need for intensive treatment.*

### Care can disappear while coverage remains

A hospital closure is more than a line on a balance sheet. It can mean
a longer drive in an emergency, a lost maternity service, or
clinicians who leave. UNC's Sheps Center tracks rural hospitals that
closed completely and those that stopped inpatient services but kept
something else. Those aren't the same, but a building that still holds
an outpatient clinic can still represent a major loss of local
capacity.[^closures]

Physician shortages are part of it. HRSA's 2025 projections anticipate
a shortage of 70,610 full-time-equivalent primary care physicians in
2038. That is a modeled future gap, not a count of missing doctors
today.[^workforce] Nationally the problem is a mix of poor
distribution, an aging workforce, thin primary care capacity, and
payment that doesn't reliably support what communities need. Closing a
hospital may sometimes be a reasonable reorganization. Keeping every
building open forever isn't the goal. Timely access to appropriate
care is.

### The financial winners are not necessarily the patients

Large healthcare companies can post big earnings while communities
lose services. UnitedHealth Group reported $447.6 billion in revenue
and $19.0 billion in operating earnings for 2025. That group includes
both insurance and Optum, so those aren't pure insurance
profits.[^earnings] Revenue isn't profit, and one good year doesn't
prove insurer profits rise forever. The problem is the incentive: a
company can improve its results without making care more accessible or
anyone healthier.

Some insurance functions are useful. Claims processing, fraud
prevention, and evidence-based coverage decisions don't become
unnecessary just because the financing is public. But patients
shouldn't depend on whether denying, delaying, or complicating their
treatment helps someone else's bottom line. A 2022 HHS Inspector
General study found some Medicare Advantage prior-authorization
denials met Medicare coverage rules.[^denials] This demonstrates some
difference between the government plan and the commercial for-profit
plan.

Existing protections have limits. The Affordable Care Act's
medical-loss-ratio rules generally require covered insurers to spend
at least 80 or 85 percent of premium revenue on medical care and
quality improvement, depending on the market, with the rest going to
administration and profit. That constrains some spending. It doesn't
guarantee affordable prices, adequate networks, or timely
appointments.[^mlr]

## Alternatives Other Countries Use

### Start with Dr. Sally Primus's comparative work

Dr. **Sally Primus**, the physician behind *Slice of Life, MD*, is an
excellent entry point through her **Healthcare Without Borders**
writing and country comparisons. She asks how specific arrangements
work: financing, physician payment, drug prices, patient costs, and
the tradeoffs a slogan hides.[^primus] Her August 2026 article
compares American primary care payment with Germany's, and asks the
useful question of whether a system that calls primary care essential
actually pays for it that way. Her September article looks at public
and private research contributions and Germany's approach to drug
pricing.[^primusdrugs] I like the method because it replaces the
search for a perfect foreign system with a search for better choices.

Universal healthcare is a goal, not a single design:

| Example | Broad arrangement | Lesson for a U.S. reform |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Canada | Provincial and territorial public insurance for medically necessary hospital and physician services, with other coverage varying | Public financing need not mean government ownership of every practice; defining the benefit matters. |
| England | Tax-funded NHS services, generally free at the point of use with statutory exceptions | Access depends on budgets, staffing, and service capacity as well as the absence of an insurance bill. |
| Germany | Statutory insurance funds alongside a private-insurance system, within a regulated framework | Multiple insurers can coexist with broadly shared rules; regulation and payment design matter. |

These are, of course, simplified descriptions, not complete accounts
of each country's system.[^canada][^nhs][^germany]

No arrangement eliminates scarcity. Public systems can have waiting
lists and multi-payer systems stay complicated. But Americans already
wait too, for authorization, a network appointment, or enough
money. Compare the barriers that actually exist, not an imperfect
public system against an imaginary private one.

## Potential Solutions

### Medicare for All should mean an improved benefit

The Medicare for All bill Sanders, Jayapal, and Dingell introduced in
2025 is a concrete starting point: comprehensive public insurance with
benefits beyond traditional Medicare and broad provider choice. It is
proposed legislation, not a program available today.[^mfa] This is a
positive direction.  Eligibility should follow the person, not their
employer, income category, or skill at navigating enrollment
paperwork. Essential coverage should include primary and specialist
care, hospital treatment, mental health, prescriptions, and meaningful
dental, vision, and hearing benefits. Long-term services and supports
need explicit treatment too. A policy shouldn't call itself
comprehensive and leave families to discover that their largest
potential expense sits outside the system.

Public insurance doesn't make every doctor a government
employee. Financing and delivering care are separate decisions. The
goal is that medically appropriate treatment is available through
participating providers without patients negotiating several private
coverage systems.

### Universal coverage should make primary care ordinary

Continuous, affordable coverage lets people seek primary care without
first asking whether they can afford the visit or whether a lost job
has cut off their eligibility. It makes a regular care relationship
and follow-up financially feasible. Universal enrollment and a
comprehensive benefit go together, since being enrolled in a plan with
unaffordable cost sharing can still leave care out of reach. While
Medicare reimbursements are arguably too low for primary care, that
is an addressable problem and the elimination of an entire layer of
insurance billing infrastructure at every medical provider should
provide some modest increase in actual provider profit even before
reimbursement changes.

The Oregon Health Insurance Experiment found that gaining Medicaid
increased use of primary and preventive care and reduced financial
strain. It studied a particular expansion, not Medicare for All, but
it supports the basic point that removing financial barriers changes
whether people get care.[^coverageaccess] The goal is appropriate use
of primary care, not fewer encounters with medicine at any cost. Newly
covered patients may need deferred exams and referrals, and seeing
more of them at first means unmet needs are being addressed.

### Fund primary care for the work we need it to do

Coverage can't open an appointment slot that doesn't exist. Primary
care has to be funded for adequate staffing, time with patients,
coordination with specialists, medication review, and follow-up
between visits, including nurses, pharmacists, and behavioral health
professionals as well as physicians. The National Academies' 2021
report recommends paying primary care more and differently, with
hybrid models that combine payment for services and prospective
support for caring for a patient population.[^primaryfunding]

I would use stable, risk-adjusted funding for continuing care
alongside payment for services, so a practice caring for people with
greater medical and social needs has the resources to do it. Access
and quality safeguards are essential so prospective payment doesn't
become one more incentive to withhold care. This is an investment in
keeping people healthier, and it shouldn't have to promise a quick
financial return to some individual payer. Risk-adjustment requires
thoughtful implementation to avoid the current prevalent schemes
to assess patients as having fictitious ailments to increase
reimbursement. That drains resources from where they're needed as
well as putting patients at risk by having false diagnoses in
their records.

### Give people a credible alternative to the emergency room

Emergency departments must stay open to anyone who needs
them. Reducing avoidable demand means timely appointments, urgent
advice, after-hours options, and continuing treatment, not
discouraging frightened or seriously ill people from seeking help.

Coverage alone doesn't guarantee fewer emergency visits. The Oregon
experiment found Medicaid coverage increased emergency-department use
during the study, even as other care increased. Affordable care
releases unmet demand; it doesn't automatically substitute primary
care for emergency care.[^oregon] The stronger claim is that
**universal coverage combined with properly funded, accessible primary
care** lets us manage more needs earlier and reduce avoidable crises
over time. Measure it through access, continuity, outcomes, and
potentially preventable emergency visits and admissions.

### Coverage must come with capacity

![Four responsibilities for a better healthcare system: universal
coverage, affordable care, available clinicians and facilities, and
accountable administration.](coverage-and-care.svg)

*Proposed design priorities. Coverage is necessary, but a benefit
cannot deliver care without clinicians, facilities, and workable
administration.*

Changing the payer while leaving delivery underfunded would repeat an old
mistake. I would pair universal coverage with:

- A primary care payment structure that funds prevention, continuity,
  team-based chronic care, and time for complicated patients.
- Workforce investment, including training and incentives to practice in
  underserved communities. The current caps on residencies need to be
  raised significantly to address impending shortages. We're already
  behind that curve.
- Hospital funding that pays for essential readiness and local access, not
  just profitable procedures.
- Negotiated drug prices and clear assessment of clinical benefit, with
  transparent appeals.
- Accessible scheduling, usable records, and adequate mental health and
  long-term care capacity.

These are design requirements, not automatic consequences of single-payer
insurance. They need funding, administration, and public evaluation.

### Pay for it honestly

Medicare for All moves a lot of spending onto public budgets. That
alone doesn't tell us whether the country spends more overall, because
taxes can replace premiums, employer payments, and deductibles. The
household question is the combined burden and the care received. A
financing plan should show its effect across income levels and family
circumstances, including employers' contributions and the chance that
compensation adjusts.

CBO's 2020 analysis of illustrative single-payer designs found
national spending could fall or rise depending on payment rates, cost
sharing, and benefits, and warned that demand could outrun
supply. Those weren't a score of the 2025 bill, and nobody should
reuse the dollar figures as guaranteed savings.[^cbo] Administrative
simplification and bargaining power are real opportunities. Newly
affordable treatment raises demand. Cutting provider payments too fast
can undermine the clinics and hospitals the reform is meant to
protect. All three belong in the same discussion. Not every preventive
service reduces lifetime spending either, and people who live longer
still need care, so the financial case should identify avoidable costs
realistically.

### Plan the transition around patients

A workable transition preserves treatment relationships, keeps
hospitals financially stable, and moves enrollment and payment in
tested stages. People who work in insurance administration deserve
retraining and income support; their livelihoods are not an accounting
adjustment. We will need published milestones, independent financial
review, and measures of real access: whether people have a regular
primary care team, appointment waits, travel distances, continuity,
potentially avoidable emergency visits and hospitalizations, financial
hardship, and disparities. Public administration should face the same
transparency we demand of private administration.

None of this happens easily. A financing structure that serves
patients will be hard to establish if incumbents can use concentrated
wealth to preserve barriers and favorable payment rules, which is the
argument in [money in politics](../money-in-politics/) and [economic
policy](../economic-policy/).  It also depends on a legislature that
actually answers to voters, which is what [the representation
article](../representation/) is about.

The case for Medicare for All isn't that government can do no
wrong. It is that care should be organized around a public obligation,
not around your bargaining position when you're sick. The first
question should be how to help people stay healthier and live well
with chronic illness. When serious treatment is needed, it should be
there. A system that repeatedly pays for rescue while neglecting the
care that could prevent some crises has its priorities backward. We
don't need a perfect system. We need to stop treating the failures in
other systems as a reason to accept failures in our own, and start
funding healthcare as deliberately as we fund sick care.
