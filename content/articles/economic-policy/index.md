Markets do useful things. They let people try ideas, discover demand, build
businesses, and challenge the established way of doing something.
Competition can reward better products and make failure less permanent.
None of that requires believing that whatever makes the most money must be
good for society.

The "greed is good" version of capitalism turns morality on its head.
It says extracting more value for an owner is sufficient justification
for a business decision, even when the costs land on workers,
customers, communities, or the environment. Greed is instead a deadly
sin, but you needn't delve into Judeo-Christian ethics to discover
how harmful it is.  Competition drives people to do better for
themselves and for society, but unregulated capitalism actually
distorts competition and rewards
[rent-seeking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rent-seeking) behavior.

**I favor a market economy with strong public guardrails: real competition,
worker bargaining power, consumer protection, and limits on turning wealth
into political control.** Profit can be a reward for serving people. It
shouldn't be an excuse for exploiting their lack of alternatives.

And let's be honest with our terms.  A competitive, capitalist economy
strongly regulated is not socialism and it's not communism.  Protecting the
vulnerable and restricting people's basest behaviors does not change the
basic economic system.

## Where We Started

### Markets have always needed a rulebook

Property rights, contracts, corporate organization, bankruptcy, patents, and
liability are all legal arrangements, and a market operates inside them.
Declining to change a rule is still a decision about whose interests the rule
serves.

Robert Reich makes this point forcefully: the so-called free market depends
on laws and rules, and powerful actors can influence those rules to
reinforce their position.[^reich] That undercuts the simple choice between
"the market" and "government interference." There is already government in
the market. The real questions are whether its rules are justified, whose
opportunities they protect, and whether the public can change them. None of
this means success is politically manufactured. People innovate and take
risks. They do it on a playing field somebody has defined.

### Competition needed protection from competitors

Businesses don't always prefer competitive markets. A firm may compete hard
until it has a protected position, then work to make competition harder. The
Sherman Act of 1890 and the later Clayton and Federal Trade Commission Acts
were an answer: constrain restraints of trade, anticompetitive conduct, and
mergers that threaten competition.[^antitrust]

Scale can bring real benefits: lower unit costs, better logistics, serious
research investment, or a service that works because many people use it. The
question is whether those benefits require letting the same firm block entry,
control dependent businesses, or dictate terms to people who can't realistically
leave.

### A slogan became a permission structure

Greed isn't new, and earlier capitalism was no golden age of fairness. What
changed is the normalization of a narrower standard, where shareholder
returns become the dominant measure and other obligations are obstacles to
get around. Reich emphasizes that we need both laws and norms. If restraint
depends entirely on executives' goodwill, a company that ignores it gains an
advantage over one that behaves responsibly.[^guardrails]

That is why asking companies to be nicer doesn't work. A durable system has
to make harmful behavior costly and beneficial behavior viable.

## Where We Are

### Success and extraction can look the same on a balance sheet

A company can raise earnings by providing better products and services
than others serving a market. It can also raise earnings by making
cancellation harder, restricting repair, raising fees on dependent
sellers, suppressing competition, or moving risk onto other
people. Every one of those improves a financial measure of a specific
entity by harming others rather than making a fair trade with others.

![A conceptual comparison: value creation improves products, quality,
 and productivity; extraction relies on lock-in, weakened
 alternatives, and shifting costs onto
 others.](value-and-extraction.svg)

*An analytical distinction, not a classification of every profitable
business. Real firms can combine productive activity with harmful
practices.*

Economists call returns sustained by a protected advantage, rather than an
open contest, rents. Some temporary advantages, such as carefully designed
intellectual-property rights, encourage investment. Others just preserve the
power to collect a toll. The policy job is telling them apart. Eliminating
every reward discourages useful effort; treating every reward as proof of
useful effort protects exploitation.

### Monopoly is not the only concentration problem

A monopolist has power as a seller. A dominant buyer has power in the other
direction, over suppliers, contractors, and workers with few alternative
customers or employers. The Treasury Department's 2022 report on
labor-market competition looked at how concentration and anticompetitive
practices weaken workers' bargaining power.[^labor] Competition policy is
about wages and mobility, not just prices at the checkout. If you have few
realistic employers, "just get another job" offers little protection. If you
must reach customers through one platform, being formally independent
doesn't mean negotiating as an equal.

This doesn't mean a large organization, a high price, or even a
monopoly position is automatically unlawful under U.S.  antitrust
law. The FTC distinguishes monopoly power gained through superior
products or business acumen from power obtained or kept through
exclusionary conduct.[^monopoly] Harmful concentrations need
a new policy response when current law allows antisocial behavior.

### Cory Doctorow describes the loss of exit

Cory Doctorow's account of **enshittification** describes a platform
pattern: attract users, build dependence, reshape the deal for
business customers, then extract more of the value for the
platform.[^doctorow] The force of the idea is the analysis of power,
not just the word. A service can get worse and stay profitable if
customers can't leave without losing contacts, audiences, purchased
media, or access to a market. I provide a description of a concrete
case in [Gmail Enshittification](../gmail-enshittification/), where
Google is removing features that I, and other people with their own
domains, have long depended on. Google's near monopoly position and
customer lock-in in the space makes it easy for them to remove
services while raising costs without significant fear of lost
business.

Doctorow's work on interoperability points toward a remedy: let people
change services without abandoning everything built on the old
one.[^interop] That principle extends well beyond social
media. Repairable equipment, transferable records, transparent
contracts, and the ability to use another supplier all strengthen a
customer's real options. Email interoperability allows people to leave
Gmail when the service gets worse. The fact they are now limiting
interoperability with other domain providers is arguably just
rent-seeking behavior.

### Economic power can buy protection for economic power

[Money in politics](../money-in-politics/) explains how large financial
resources influence public agendas without anyone buying a vote. Here the
process feeds itself. A protected market position generates returns. Those
returns finance lobbying, election spending, and litigation to preserve
favorable rules. The rules then sustain the position.

![A conceptual feedback loop: market power creates resources;
resources support political influence; influence can shape favorable
rules; favorable rules can reinforce market power. Guardrails target
each link.](economic-power-loop.svg)

*A mechanism to examine, not evidence that every company follows it or
that any particular contribution caused a policy decision.*

Elizabeth Warren's speeches on corporate monopolies tie competition
directly to democratic power: concentrated businesses can dominate
markets and weaken the public's ability to govern them.[^warren] I
share that concern.  Oligarchy isn't simply a society that contains
rich people. It is one where a small, wealthy group can
disproportionately decide the rules everyone else lives under. The
same dynamic of insiders writing their own rules shows up in [how we
draw districts and run elections](../representation/).

## Alternatives and the Principles They Suggest

### Preserve markets while changing the terms

We don't have to choose between leaving every decision to concentrated
private power and having the state direct every transaction. Public
rules can protect the competitive process while businesses decide what
to offer and consumers decide what to buy. A safety standard applies
to every supplier. A fair labor rule keeps responsible employers from
being undercut by abusive ones. An interoperability requirement lets a
newcomer offer a service without rebuilding a whole network.

These interventions need evidence and proportion. A rule with enormous
fixed costs can protect the biggest firms by making entry harder. A
consumer protection nobody can enforce accomplishes little. The goal
isn't regulation measured by pages. It is a market where competing and
leaving remain realistic, and where third parties don't bear costs
nobody accounts for.

### Recognize where competition is insufficient

Some services don't fit a consumer-choice model. Nobody comparison
shops during a medical emergency, and a community gains little from
duplicating every utility network just to have competing
providers. There, public provision, regulated rates, or
universal-service obligations may be the better tool. The [healthcare
article](../healthcare/) makes this concrete: universal public
insurance can coexist with many kinds of providers, and the fact that
a market can organize one part of a service doesn't settle how to
finance the whole public obligation. Similarly, utility competition
among providers in the gas and electric industries along with
regulated monopolies for distribution can be beneficial. Having
multiple duplicative, expensive distribution networks provides little
benefit to anyone, but increases costs. Careful thought to
first-principles is required though. For example a government enforced
monopoly for "cable" providers for a geographic area can prevent
expensive duplication, but if the monopolist is not required to
provide access to that infrastructure to other providers the monopolist
will almost certainly extract fees much higher than they would if
competition was enforced.

## Potential Solutions

### Make competition policy about practical power

I would strengthen antitrust enforcement against exclusionary behavior and
look hard at mergers for their effects on customers, workers, suppliers, and
future entrants. The Clayton Act's merger standard is preventive; it reaches
deals that may substantially lessen competition or tend to create monopoly.
Waiting until every consequence can be measured can mean waiting until the
rival is gone.[^mergers]

Enforcement still needs a theory of harm, evidence, and due process. Size
alone is not an analysis, and a claim of efficiency shouldn't excuse a
structure that gives one company control of a whole market. Remedies should
match the problem. Sometimes banning a practice is enough. Sometimes a
dominant firm keeps evading behavioral conditions and structural separation
makes more sense. Breaking up a business should be a reasoned remedy for
concentration or conflicts of interest, not a punishment for success.

### Restore the ability to leave

We must have usable data portability, appropriate interoperability,
and a practical right to repair. An export button that produces
unusable files is not portability. An appliance serviceable only
through the manufacturer's chosen channel is worth less than the sales
contract suggests. Interoperability has to protect security and
privacy too; giving buyers a choice shouldn't mean handing every other
buyer's information to unaccountable third parties.[^interop]

The test is simple: can a person or business change providers without
paying an unreasonable penalty in lost relationships, records,
purchases, or working equipment?

### Give workers and smaller businesses bargaining power

Competition among employers matters, but it isn't the only
safeguard. We need to defend collective bargaining, enforce wage and
safety rules, and address practices that needlessly restrict job
mobility. Smaller suppliers need fair access to customers as well. A
platform that runs a marketplace while competing with the businesses
on it almost certainly has a conflict that is harmful. Transparency,
nondiscrimination, or separation may be justified depending on the
market.[^warren] Nobody is owed a profit. But dependence shouldn't
become a license to impose terms the other side can't refuse.

### Stop treating shifted costs as efficiency

An operation can look efficient because it sends pollution downstream,
leaves workers uninsured, or hands long-term risk to the public. That
is cost shifting, however good the accounts look. We must strengthen
liability, environmental standards, consumer protection, and financial
safeguards so businesses carry a fairer share of the risks they
create. Walmart should not be able to escape the costs of providing
medical coverage to their employees simply because they're the only
real employer in an area.

Taxes should support the public foundations of enterprise, meaning
education, infrastructure, courts, research, and basic security, among
others without letting the most powerful actors escape obligations
that ordinary households and smaller firms carry. There are tradeoffs
to every method of funding services, but fairness, transparency and
competition should be forefront.  Opportunities for rent-seeking
should be minimized. [Healthcare]({{< relref healthcare >}}) is an
example where eliminating most of the health insurance industry can
likely improve the system for all the participants while eliminating
or vastly reducing the costs now extracted as insurance profits.

### Break the political feedback loop

Competition rules stay vulnerable if the people they bind can dominate
the process that writes and enforces them. I would pair economic
reform with [campaign-finance reform](../money-in-politics/), lobbying
transparency, conflict rules, and enough resources for independent
enforcement, and with [a Supreme Court](../supreme-court/) whose
members are held to enforceable ethical standards. Regulators should
be watched for capture as well as incompetence. A company shouldn't
get favorable treatment because it funds the right allies, or face
retaliation because its owners back the other side.  A government
strong enough to restrain concentrated business power is strong enough
to misuse it.

### Measure whether people's options improve

Stock prices and aggregate growth are useful, but they don't show who
gained bargaining power or who lost access to an essential service. We
should track entry by new competitors, switching costs, real wages and
mobility, service quality, complaints, and the distribution of
gains. Where a market needs special oversight, reliability and
universal access belong in the assessment.

Reich, Warren, and Doctorow come at these issues from different
directions.  Together they make a useful point: bad outcomes often
follow from rules that let concentrated power escape meaningful
constraint. Competition is valuable when it gives people choices and
rewards useful work. It is much less valuable as a slogan defending an
incumbent against the rules needed to keep competition alive. Greed
can explain behavior. It shouldn't be the principle by which we decide
whether that behavior deserves society's protection.

Lastly, let us not confuse economic well-being with well-being in
general.  Money can measurable improve one's life.  But there is
abundant evidence that it does not produce happiness or a long, healthy
life by itself.  Moreover, a society with deep economic inequality
suffers even more in terms of real health and happiness. We **must**
begin to focus more on the real people within our political systems.
